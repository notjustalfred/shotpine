How to Make Screenshots Look Good (Without Opening Figma)

August 29, 2026

A raw screenshot does its job, but it rarely sells anything. Hard edges, a random crop, whatever was behind your window — it reads as "I pasted this in a hurry." A few small adjustments turn the same capture into something that looks at home on a landing page, in a tweet, or on a slide. Here's the actual workflow, and why each step matters.

1. Put it on a background

The single biggest upgrade is getting your screenshot off the bare page and onto a background. A soft gradient or a clean solid color gives the image a frame and a mood. Match the background to your brand colors and your screenshots start to look like a set instead of a pile of one-offs.

One honest caveat: the pastel purple-to-pink gradient with a big blur got overused a few years ago, and people can spot it from across a feed. Reach for a calmer solid or a gradient that actually echoes your product's palette. The goal is polish, not a cliché.

2. Add padding — give it room to breathe

Once there's a background, don't let the screenshot fill it edge to edge. Add padding so the image sits inside the color with margin around it. Somewhere in the 40–80px range usually looks right. Padding is what makes the difference between "screenshot with a colored border" and "designed image."

3. Round the corners

Sharp 90-degree corners look like a raw OS capture. A modest corner radius softens the whole thing and matches how modern app UIs already look. Keep it subtle — you want rounded, not a bubble.

4. Drop a shadow

A soft drop shadow lifts the screenshot off the background so it feels like an object sitting on a surface rather than a sticker pasted flat. Keep the shadow diffuse and low-contrast; a harsh black shadow undoes all the polish you just added.

5. (Optional) Wrap it in a window frame

If you're showing a web app or a site, dropping the screenshot inside a browser frame or a macOS window chrome adds instant context — the viewer immediately reads it as "a real product on a real screen." Use it when the frame adds meaning; skip it when the UI already speaks for itself.

6. (Optional) Tilt it

A slight 3D tilt adds depth and movement, which helps in a busy social feed where everything else is flat and rectangular. Use this one sparingly — a small angle looks intentional, a big one looks like a gimmick.

7. Export at the right size

Finally, match the output to where it's going. A square-ish frame for Instagram, a wider ratio for a Twitter/X card or a hero image, a tighter crop for docs. Exporting a preset aspect ratio saves you re-cropping later, and exporting at 2× (retina) keeps the image crisp on high-density screens instead of looking soft.
